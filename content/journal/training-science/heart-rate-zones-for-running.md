> **Quick summary**
>
> - **Five zones:** Zone 1 (50–60% of max heart rate) up to Zone 5 (90–100%).
> - **Estimate max heart rate:** 208 − 0.7 × age is more accurate for most adults than 220 − age. Either can be 10 bpm or more off for an individual.
> - **Example, age 40:** estimated max 180 bpm; Zone 2 is about 108–126 bpm.
> - **Most runs should be easy:** roughly 80% of weekly running in Zones 1–2, the rest in Zones 3–5.
> - **Better accuracy:** use your resting heart rate (the heart-rate reserve method) or a supervised field test.

Every running watch shows heart rate zones, but the default settings are often wrong for you, and many runners spend their easy days too high and their hard days not high enough. This guide explains what the zones mean, how to set yours properly, and which runs belong in each one.

## What heart rate zones are

Heart rate zones divide your effort into bands based on how hard your heart is working. The most common model uses five zones, each a percentage of your maximum heart rate (max HR):

| Zone | % of max HR | Feels like | Main purpose |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Zone 1 | 50–60% | Very easy; full conversation | Recovery, warm-ups and cool-downs |
| Zone 2 | 60–70% | Easy; comfortable conversation | Aerobic base, most easy and long runs |
| Zone 3 | 70–80% | Steady; short sentences | Steady runs; marathon pace for many recreational runners |
| Zone 4 | 80–90% | Comfortably hard; a few words | Threshold and tempo runs |
| Zone 5 | 90–100% | Very hard; no talking | Short intervals and race finishes |

Different watches and coaches use slightly different boundaries, and some use three or seven zones. What matters is that the zones are set from your own numbers and used consistently.

## Step 1: estimate your max heart rate

Your zones are only as good as your max HR. Max HR is largely set by genetics and declines with age; it is not a measure of fitness, and a higher number doesn't mean a fitter runner.

There are two common formulas:

| Formula | Calculation | Age 40 example | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Classic | 220 − age | 180 bpm | Simple, widely used; tends to overestimate in younger adults and underestimate in older adults |
| Tanaka | 208 − 0.7 × age | 180 bpm | Derived from a large analysis of studies; generally a better estimate across ages |

At age 40 both give 180 bpm, but they drift apart at other ages. At 25, the classic formula gives 195 and Tanaka gives 190; at 60, it's 160 versus 166.

The important caveat: any age formula is an average. Your real max HR can easily be 10–15 bpm higher or lower. If your zones feel wrong, for example if Zone 2 feels like a struggle or Zone 4 feels easy, your estimate is probably off.

## Step 2 (more accurate): the heart-rate reserve method

The heart-rate reserve method, also called the Karvonen method, adds your resting heart rate. Because resting heart rate falls as aerobic fitness improves, it personalises your zones.

**Formula:** target heart rate = resting HR + (max HR − resting HR) × zone %

**How to measure resting heart rate:** take your pulse for a full minute, or use your watch's reading, first thing in the morning before getting up. Use the average of three to five mornings.

**Worked example:** age 40, estimated max HR 180, resting HR 55. The reserve is 180 − 55 = 125 bpm.

| Zone | % of reserve | Calculation | Zone range (bpm) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Zone 1 | 50–60% | 55 + 125 × 0.50 to 0.60 | 118–130 |
| Zone 2 | 60–70% | 55 + 125 × 0.60 to 0.70 | 130–142 |
| Zone 3 | 70–80% | 55 + 125 × 0.70 to 0.80 | 142–155 |
| Zone 4 | 80–90% | 55 + 125 × 0.80 to 0.90 | 155–168 |
| Zone 5 | 90–100% | 55 + 125 × 0.90 to 1.00 | 168–180 |

Notice that these zones sit higher than the percentage-of-max zones for the same 40-year-old: Zone 2 is 130–142 bpm instead of 108–126 bpm. Many runners find heart-rate-reserve zones match how easy runs actually feel. Pick one method and use it consistently; don't mix them.

## Step 3 (most accurate): test your max heart rate

A lab test or a supervised field test gives a measured max HR rather than an estimate. A common field test:

1. Warm up for 15 minutes of easy running, including a few short accelerations.
2. Run 3 minutes hard on a gentle hill or track.
3. Jog easily for 2 minutes.
4. Run 3 minutes as hard as you can sustain, finishing with an all-out final 30 seconds.
5. The highest reading in the final minute, from a chest strap, is close to your max HR.

**Safety first:** maximal efforts put real stress on the heart. Only do this test if you're healthy, already training regularly, and have had no chest pain, dizziness or heart symptoms. If you are over 40, are new to exercise, or have a medical condition, speak to a doctor before any all-out test. Stop immediately if you feel unwell.

## Which runs belong in each zone

| Run type | Target zone | Typical share of weekly running |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recovery run | Zone 1 | Part of the easy 80% |
| Easy run | Zone 2 | Most of the easy 80% |
| Long run | Zone 2, drifting into low Zone 3 late on | Part of the easy 80% |
| Marathon-pace run | Zone 3 for most recreational runners | Part of the hard 20% |
| Tempo or threshold run | Zone 4 | Part of the hard 20% |
| Intervals (800 m–1.6 km repeats) | Zone 4–5 | Part of the hard 20% |

Studies of elite endurance athletes consistently find that around 80% of their training is done at low intensity. Recreational runners tend to do the opposite, running most sessions in a moderate "grey zone" that is too hard to recover from and too easy to build speed. If your training plan lists paces, like our [sub-4 hour marathon training plan](/journal/marathon-training/sub-4-hour-marathon-training-plan/), use heart rate on easy and long runs to keep them genuinely easy. Our guide to [long-run pace](/journal/marathon-training/marathon-long-run-pace/) explains why slower long runs build more fitness.

## Zone 2 running: why it feels so slow

Most runners who start training in Zone 2 are surprised by how slowly they have to run, sometimes walking up hills to stay in zone. That's normal.

- **Why it works:** Zone 2 builds the aerobic system (more capillaries, more mitochondria, better fat use) with little fatigue, so you can run more without breaking down.
- **What to expect:** many runners find their pace at the same heart rate gradually improves over several weeks to a few months of consistent easy running.
- **Make it easier:** run on flat routes, slow down early before heart rate climbs, and use a chest strap so you aren't reacting to sensor spikes.

Zone 2 isn't the only zone that matters. Your plan still needs the hard 20%: tempo runs, intervals and marathon-pace work.

## Why is my heart rate high on easy runs?

If your heart rate jumps out of Zone 2 even when running slowly, check these common causes:

| Cause | What happens | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heat and humidity | Heart rate rises to cool you; it can be 10–20 bpm higher at the same pace | Run by heart rate and accept a slower pace. See [running in hot weather](/journal/marathon-training/marathon-training-in-hot-weather/) |
| Cardiac drift | Heart rate creeps up during long runs as you sweat and warm up | Normal; slow slightly and drink to thirst |
| Dehydration | Lower blood volume makes the heart beat faster | Drink normally through the day; see our [fueling plan](/journal/marathon-training/marathon-fueling-plan/) |
| Poor sleep, stress or illness | Heart rate runs higher than usual | Take an easy day or rest |
| Caffeine | Can raise heart rate slightly in some people | Note your pattern |
| Wrist sensor error | Optical sensors can lock onto your cadence or lag | Use a chest strap, or ignore readings in the first 5–10 minutes |
| Zones set too low | An age formula underestimated your max HR | Use the reserve method or a field test |

## How to set up zones on your watch

1. Open your watch or app's heart rate zone settings (often under user profile or physiological metrics).
2. Enter your max HR (estimated or tested) and your resting HR.
3. Choose the calculation method: "% max HR" or "% heart rate reserve" (sometimes called HRR).
4. Check the zone boundaries match your chosen method, and adjust if your watch uses different percentages.
5. Add a heart rate alert for easy runs, set to the top of your Zone 2.

## FAQ

### What heart rate zone should I run in?

Most runners should do around 80% of their running in Zones 1–2 (easy), with the remaining 20% in Zones 3–5 for marathon-pace runs, tempo runs and intervals.

### What is a good heart rate for running?

There's no single good number. It depends on your max heart rate, which is mostly set by age and genetics. For easy runs, aim for Zone 2: about 60–70% of max heart rate, or 108–126 bpm for a typical 40-year-old.

### How do I calculate my max heart rate?

The simplest estimates are 220 − age or the more accurate 208 − 0.7 × age. Both can be off by 10 bpm or more, so a supervised field or lab test gives a more reliable number.

### Is 220 minus age accurate?

It's a rough estimate. It tends to overestimate max heart rate in younger adults and underestimate it in older adults. The Tanaka formula (208 − 0.7 × age) is generally closer across age groups.

### Why does my heart rate go up when I run slowly?

Common causes are heat, humidity, dehydration, poor sleep, illness and cardiac drift on long runs. Wrist-based sensors can also give false high readings; a chest strap is more accurate.

### How long does it take to see results from Zone 2 training?

Many runners notice their pace at the same heart rate improving after several weeks to a few months of consistent easy running. Progress depends on how much you run and how consistent you are.

### Should I train by heart rate or pace?

Both are useful. Heart rate is best for keeping easy and long runs easy, especially in heat or on hills. Pace is better for structured workouts like intervals and marathon-pace runs, because heart rate lags behind changes in effort.

*This article is general information, not medical advice. Heart rate targets may not apply if you take medication that affects heart rate (such as beta blockers) or have a heart condition. If you experience chest pain, unusual breathlessness, dizziness or palpitations, stop exercising and seek medical advice.*
